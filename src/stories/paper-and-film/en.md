---
title: Why every photo sits on paper
summary: On white borders, and the difference between an image that flows past and one that's been kept.
date: 2026-08-21
---

Photos in this app don't fill the screen. Each one sits inside a white paper border, like a print. At first glance it reads as decoration. It's actually the most deliberate design decision in the app.

## An image that flows vs. an image that's kept

The same photo carries different weight in different places. In a camera roll it's one of thousands, part of a stream your thumb moves through without stopping. Lying on a desk as a print, it's an object at rest. Someone chose it. Someone decided it was worth keeping. That's why it's there.

A record built from one photo a day shouldn't look like the stream. The photo you chose today is not passing through — it was selected, and the screen should say so. That's what the white border is: a mark that separates a kept image from a consumed one.

## Filters as the temperature of memory

There are five film-derived filters — a warm tone, a soft grain, a vintage stock, and two classic looks — or you can leave the photo untouched.

They do the opposite of what "enhancement" usually means. Nothing gets smoothed or brightened for presentation. Grain is added, colors settle and fade slightly, corners darken. The photo shifts from "an image just captured" toward "a memory some time has passed over."

Memory doesn't store things at full resolution anyway. It keeps them a little warmer and a little faded. The filters simply set that temperature in advance.

## One frame makes you look twice

When there's no limit on how much you can capture, nothing gets a second look. But give a day exactly one frame, and the question changes: out of everything today, which moment is worth it? You scan the day once before answering.

That single pause — brief, almost unnoticeable — is the entire mechanism. The photo you keep after asking the question stays with you longer than a hundred kept without it, not because it's a better photo, but because choosing it required you to look at your own day.

## Paper, on a screen

Somewhere along the way photos stopped being things you hold. There's a moment of taking, and no moment of touching.

The paper frame is partly an homage to that missing moment. The photo of your day, resting on a sheet of paper — on a screen, yes, but resting rather than streaming. Flip through the calendar and those sheets accumulate date by date, the way a drawer fills with prints. What keeps a record going, we suspect, is less any feature than an image like that in the back of your mind.
